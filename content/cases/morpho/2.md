## Summary

When depositing into a vault, it is worth checking who controls the vault, who created the markets it allocates into, and who controls the assets used as collateral.

Those can look like separate parts of the system while ultimately depending on the same, or closely related, parties.

AZND/USDC is an interesting example of this. Depositor USDC was routed through a curated vault into a market where AZND was used as collateral. A single borrower was then able to take almost all of the available USDC against that collateral. The unresolved question is whether AZND's asserted dollar value fairly represented the value of the real-world assets behind it.

## Context

> Skip this if you already know how Morpho works.

There are three useful pieces to keep in mind when thinking about Morpho: **Assets**, **Markets** and **Vaults**.

**Assets** can be used permissionlessly. Anyone creating a market can choose its loan asset and collateral asset.

**Markets** are also permissionless. A market creator chooses the assets, LLTV, interest-rate model and, importantly here, the oracle used to value the collateral.

**Vaults** sit one level above markets. Allocators choose which markets vault capital can enter and therefore which assets and market configurations depositors are exposed to.

> **Important take away:** The relevant risk is whether the asset, market and vault are independently controlled. Common control is not yet conclusively established in this case.

## Example

[AZND/USDC](https://app.morpho.org/ethereum/variable/0xfd0d72a4f0469598b566b1bc5fe64835f828f90b1fb7d746148c086164cd4cc2/aznd-usdc) was a Morpho market using:

- [USDC](https://etherscan.io/token/0xA0b86991c6218b36c1d19d4a2e9eb0ce3606eb48) as the loan asset
- [AZND](https://etherscan.io/token/0x52c66b5e7f8fde20843de900c5c8b4b0f23708a0) as collateral

There was also [Alpha USDC Asia V2](https://app.morpho.org/ethereum/vault/0x35Cbe8542E70fa2f7F9cDF129F19e593F4b4f560/alpha-usdc-asia-v2), a Morpho vault curated by AlphaPing, which allocated USDC into the market. Morpho described it as:

> “Alpha USDC Asia V2 is a USD-denominated lending vault focused on allocating capital to overcollateralized credit markets with exposure to Asia-linked Real-World Assets RWA.”

[Mu Digital publicly promoted](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/mu-digital-alphaping-transformed-asia-credit-new-defi-base-o6ygc) the broader AZND/Morpho rollout, including Alpha USDC Asia V2 and strategies involving AZND.

At one point, the market had roughly 7.734m USDC supplied and almost exactly the same amount borrowed. Almost all of the supply came from Alpha USDC Asia V2 through its Morpho adapter.

One EOA, [`0xe38a...Dc4C`](https://etherscan.io/address/0xe38a653bAb0a52A1166B4e7B78d79aa68067Dc4C), represented effectively all of the borrowing against around 9.65m AZND collateral.

Worth noting as well, there was a hard-coded AZND/USDC oracle price, which should be immediately suspicious.

![USDC depositors supplied the Alpha USDC Asia V2 vault, curated by AlphaPing, which allocated to the AZND/USDC Morpho market where one large borrower EOA held effectively all of the borrow. The relationship between the entities is marked as unconfirmed.](/content/cases/morpho/2/alphaping-flow.png)

The central uncertainty is whether that one-dollar claim was a fair representation of AZND's value. Because AZND represented an RWA, answering that question requires information about the offchain assets, legal claims and redemption process rather than the Morpho market alone.

If AZND could reliably be redeemed or sold for one dollar, borrowing USDC against it would not necessarily be a problem. It would be collateralised borrowing against a real-world asset.

If it could not, a borrower received USDC against collateral that may have been worth less than the market assumed, potentially leaving the original vault depositors exposed.

## How to address

The first thing to check is concentration. Look at who holds the collateral and whether most of the market's supply or borrowing sits with one vault, address or related group.

A vault curator also being the market deployer is not automatically a problem. It matters when that party may also control the collateral and most of the market's volume. These risks stack, so a new asset in a new market needs much stronger evidence before a vault allocates to it.

A market between established, well-understood assets is generally easier to underwrite. Even then, its configuration and concentration still need to be checked.

For every significant market a vault can allocate into, check:

1. How concentrated are the collateral holdings?
2. How concentrated are the market's supply and borrow positions?
3. Are the loan and collateral assets established and independently understood?
4. Is the market new or does it have a meaningful operating history?
5. Who owns and curates the vault, who created the market and who controls the collateral?
6. Does one party control several of those layers or most of the market's volume?
7. What gives the collateral its value outside the protocol?
8. Are any prices, caps or conversion rates hard-coded, and what justifies those values?

The AZND/USDC market's fixed one-dollar oracle is one example of a hard-coded assumption. That is not automatically unsafe, but it is a red flag that needs an economic explanation.
